### s2 Too many rules（章节 problem）
| 句号 | English |
|---|---|
| l09 | Start with an ordinary computer program. People write the rules, one by one, and the computer follows them. |
| l10 | For payroll, or booking a flight, the rules are crystal clear, and that works fine. |
| l11 | But some things have rules nobody can spell out. Like recognizing a handwritten three. |
| l12 | Every one of these is a three, written by a real person. Some are fat, some are thin, some slant. You recognize them at a glance. |
| l13 | But try writing down how. Two bumps, one on top of the other? So does an eight. Opens to the left at the bottom? So does a five. |
| l14 | However many rules you write, some threes slip through, and some other digits get mistaken for threes. |

### s3 A different approach
| 句号 | English |
|---|---|
| l15 | Most of today's AI takes a different approach. Don't write rules. Give examples. |
| l16 | Show the computer hundreds, even thousands, of handwritten digits, each one with the answer: this is a three, this is a seven. |
| l17 | It finds the pattern in the examples on its own. That approach is called machine learning. |
| l18 | What it learns is called a model. Hand the model a new image, and it gives you its answer. |
| l19 | So behind AI there isn't a programmer typing in answers one by one. Most of what it can do, it learned from data. |

### s4 An analogy（章节 analogy）
| 句号 | English |
|---|---|
| l20 | It's like teaching a child their numbers. You don't hand them a manual that says: a three is two half circles, stacked. |
| l21 | You just point and say, again and again: this is a three. After enough of them, they get it. They can read it even in handwriting they've never seen. |
| l22 | The child is the model. What you point at are the examples, with their answers. |
| l23 | Two things don't match, though. A model usually needs far more examples than a child. And the pattern it learns is often something even its makers can't spell out. |
| l24 | Or think about cooking. One cook follows a recipe, step by step. Another has tasted a thousand dishes, and works out the recipe on their own. |
| l25 | A traditional program cooks from the recipe. Machine learning writes the recipe, from a thousand dishes. |

### s5 Let's run an experiment（章节 experiment）
| 句号 | English |
|---|---|
| l26 | Let's actually try it. This is a public dataset of handwritten digits. 1,797 images, each just eight by eight squares. |
| l27 | First, we lock away three hundred of them. The model never sees them during training. They're the exam. The rest are the examples it learns from. |
| l28 | Take a guess: with just ten examples, how much of the exam would it get right? |
| l29 | Pick ten at random, and there are usually a few digits it hasn't seen even once. It scores about 37%. That beats random guessing at 10%, but it's still a long way off. |
| l30 | Show it a hundred examples: 88%. |
| l31 | Show it all of them, almost fifteen hundred: 97%. |
| l32 | At no point did anyone tell it what a three looks like. The more it sees, the better it gets. Your photo app finds your cat the same way. |
| l33 | Still, out of three hundred exam questions, it missed ten. A few of them would make a person hesitate too. |

### s6 A prediction machine（章节 lens）
| 句号 | English |
|---|---|
| l34 | Here's another way to see it. Today's AI is something like a prediction machine. |
| l35 | Give it something, and it guesses an answer, with a level of confidence. For this image, it says: 96% sure, it's a three. |
| l36 | Give it a photo, and it guesses whether there's a cat. Give it audio, and it guesses which words were said. |
| l37 | Give it half a sentence, and it guesses the next word. Guess word after word, and you get a whole reply. The chatbots you talk to, large language models, are built on this kind of guessing. They're just extremely good at it. |
| l38 | These terms nest inside each other. AI is the biggest circle, and rules that people write count as AI too. Machine learning sits inside it. |
| l39 | Inside that is deep learning: learning with models built from many layers of simple calculations, called neural networks. Large language models are one kind of deep learning. |
| l40 | When people say AI today, they mostly mean machine learning. |

### s7 Real-world impact（章节 real）
| 句号 | English |
|---|---|
| l41 | This ability to learn from data is already changing the real world. |
| l42 | Working out the shape of a protein used to take lab work, often for years. AlphaFold learned from known structures to predict shapes directly, and the two people who led its development shared half of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. |
| l43 | ChatGPT was estimated to have passed a hundred million monthly active users about two months after launch. |
| l44 | But patterns aren't the only thing it learns. According to Reuters, Amazon once tried using AI to screen résumés. But that AI learned from ten years of résumés sent to the company, mostly from men. |
| l45 | So it started marking down résumés with the word "women's," as in "women's chess club captain." The tool was later scrapped. |
| l46 | Whatever is in the data, it learns. Bias included. |

### s8 Three things to keep in mind（章节 fix）
| 句号 | English |
|---|---|
| l47 | So when you use AI, keep three things in mind. |
| l48 | One: it only knows what it has learned. We showed it the letter A. It has never seen a letter, and it can only pick from zero to nine, so it says, with a straight face: 92% sure, it's a four. |
| l49 | Two: it gives predictions, not truth. Remember that scrawled two? It was 81% sure it was a one, and it was wrong. |
| l50 | Three: its strengths and its flaws both come from data. To judge whether an AI is trustworthy, first ask what data it learned from. |
