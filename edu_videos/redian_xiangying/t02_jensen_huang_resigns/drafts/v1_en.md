# t02 Jensen Huang resigns — English voice-over v1

## s0 Cold open

- l01: November 12, 2026. Nvidia files an 8-K: co-founder Jensen Huang is out as president, CEO and director.
- l02: Effective immediately, after 33 years. No reason given.
- l03: The stock opened down more than ten percent, and closed down 4.34%.
- l04: On November 8, he'd told staff: "I was unfaithful to my wife." With whom, he didn't say.
- l05: A private matter. Until Nvidia's board, three congressional committees and China's Foreign Ministry stepped in.
- l06: Our take: the US-China tech war turned one CEO's private life into a national security issue.
- l07: Not that he quit over national security. Nobody knows why he quit.

## s1 What's confirmed, and what's only reported

- l08: Confirmed: his letter admits infidelity.
- l09: The board opened an independent probe over reports he was a target of "foreign intelligence activity."
- l10: So far, it says, there's no sign national security or company secrets were harmed.
- l11: Then he resigned, reason unstated.
- l12: Only reported: on October 29, The New York Times cited unnamed sources.
- l13: They said US intelligence suspected a businesswoman close to him of working for Chinese intelligence.
- l14: She's a former executive at a Singapore data center company, an Nvidia customer.
- l15: On November 11, the Times cited five unnamed current and former employees.
- l16: They described another relationship, with an executive who reported to him. Nvidia declined to comment.
- l17: China's Foreign Ministry calls the spy claims groundless smears.
- l18: Singapore's Ministry of Home Affairs says it never investigated her for espionage.
- l19: Its police did probe her over false server-purchase declarations. In February: insufficient evidence.
- l20: Nobody's been charged. And is his letter about her? He didn't say. We don't know.

## s2 How a private matter became a national security issue

- l21: Why the alarms? Nvidia sells AI chips, and who gets the best ones is the front line of the tech war.
- l22: Its annual report says it's "effectively foreclosed" from China's data center market.
- l23: Washington fears chips slipping into China via Southeast Asia.
- l24: That Singapore company is under investigation in both countries over just that.
- l25: It denies wrongdoing. Nvidia says it checked those claims last year and they're untrue. No conclusion yet.
- l26: As we see it, the chain runs: critical chips, a customer suspected of rerouting them, someone close to the CEO.
- l27: That puts a private matter under a national security magnifying glass.
- l28: And look at the timeline. Every move hangs on that hook.
- l29: The board cited the intelligence reports.
- l30: Three Republican committee chairmen requested a closed-door briefing, citing national security.
- l31: The day he resigned, John Moolenaar, chairman of the House Select Committee on China, demanded security-first leadership at Nvidia.
- l32: Plus fast bipartisan export controls in the year-end defense bill, the NDAA.
- l33: Even China's Foreign Ministry told the US to stop "fabricating a so-called 'spy narrative'" as a pretext for a tech blockade.
- l34: Our read: both sides agree it's about chips.

## s3 Both parties use it to press

- l35: Our read on Washington: lawmakers in both parties want to tighten chip sales to China. The White House is loosening.
- l36: Huang stood with the White House. So both parties pounced.
- l37: Tightening: in October 2025, the Senate passed a curb on advanced chips for China, 77 to 20.
- l38: This March, Republican Senator Jim Banks and Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren asked Commerce to freeze and fully review Nvidia's advanced chip licenses.
- l39: For China and several Southeast Asian countries.
- l40: Loosening: in August 2025, Trump cleared an Nvidia chip for China, if Nvidia would "pay us as a country something."
- l41: After the scandal, he called Huang "a very honorable man."
- l42: Then Senate Democratic leader Chuck Schumer asked if officials were told not to investigate because of Trump's ties to Huang.
- l43: Republicans targeted Commerce. Senate Banking Committee Chairman Tim Scott wants Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick to testify publicly.
- l44: House Foreign Affairs Committee Chairman Brian Mast wants its export office probed. Moolenaar pushes legislation.
- l45: Not so neat, though. Warren backs a Republican tightening bill too.
- l46: The one clean line, we'd say: Democrats name Trump. Republicans don't.

## s4 A casualty of partisan warfare?

- l47: A popular line in Chinese commentary: Huang was a casualty of America's partisan war.
- l48: According to Hong Kong outlet HK01, Hu Xijin, the former Global Times editor, said this on November 9.
- l49: Democrats and the establishment were going after Huang, and through him, Trump.
- l50: A day later, he called Huang a political casualty of vicious two-party fighting.
- l51: Notice: on day one, he was aiming at the Democrats.
- l52: The record? Before November 11, the only lawmaker we found publicly urging him out was Republican Senator Kevin Cramer.
- l53: Trump, a Republican, backed him throughout.
- l54: House Minority Leader Hakeem Jeffries said resigning "was the right thing to do." But only afterward.
- l55: No record shows either party pushing him out.
- l56: The board had its own reasons: his admission, the second report, a shareholder class action filed November 9.
- l57: Our conclusion: "someone sacrificed him on purpose" doesn't hold up. "He paid for someone else's fight"? Only partly.

## s5 The pushback

- l58: The strongest pushback: national security is noise. This is plain corporate governance.
- l59: Admitted infidelity, a reported relationship with a subordinate, a shareholder lawsuit.
- l60: The board accepted his resignation November 11, the day that second report broke.
- l61: Any CEO might go over that. In 2018, Intel's CEO resigned over a relationship with an employee that broke company policy.
- l62: And the spy story? Anonymous sources, no charges, China denies it, Singapore says no espionage probe.
- l63: We agree. That's why we never said he left over national security. The direct cause may well be governance.
- l64: But why did one CEO's affair reach three congressional committees, the year-end defense bill and China's Foreign Ministry?
- l65: Intel's didn't. We think the difference isn't what he did. It's what his company sells.
- l66: Even if the spy claims prove false, it's already a national security issue, because every side treats it as one.

## s6 What sticks

- l67: Here's what we think sticks.
- l68: The more critical a company's product, the less private its leader's private life.
- l69: He doesn't draw the line between private and public. What his company sells does.
- l70: Watch November 17: Nvidia's first earnings without Jensen Huang.
- l71: His trip to Congress is still being negotiated.
- l72: The board's probe and the US probe of that Singapore company are still open.
- l73: All as of November 14. Not investment advice.
